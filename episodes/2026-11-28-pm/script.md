**One idea:** in a cyber case, the sections in the FIR decide what happens — the 7-year line (a notice is the rule, arrest
the exception) and bail or no bail — plus the two things specific to cyber cases: the seized phone, and the video-call
"arrest" that is always fake.
Why this topic today: most people named in a cyber complaint don't know that for offences up to 7 years the Supreme Court
calls a notice the rule and arrest the exception; this week's 7-day "digital arrest" in Saharanpur (₹66 lakh, reported
27 Nov) shows the one "arrest" that is always fake. The owner set the topic on 27 Nov.

Accuracy notes: arrest is never shown as forbidden — s.35(1)(b) allows it with reasons in writing, and the reel says
"isn't automatic", "the exception", "needs reasons in writing"; the notice is shown as a duty (s.35(4)), and ignoring it as
a ground on which police may arrest (s.35(6)) — nothing suggests refusing lawful process; "not on WhatsApp" is the Supreme
Court's direction on how s.35 notices are served (Jul 2025, reaffirmed 15 Jan 2026), shown next to "appear", never as a
reason to ignore a notice; "most" IT Act offences carry 3 years or less (66F, 67A, 67B, 69, 70 are the exceptions); s.77B
makes 3-year IT Act offences bailable, and BNS s.318(4) (up to 7 years) is non-bailable — the card says "bail isn't
automatic", not "no bail"; s.78 (Inspector or above) is today's law, and the reel notes that a change is being discussed
(ThePrint, 14 Nov 2026); the seizure list is described as "signed by witnesses" (s.103(5), s.105) without promising a copy
in every case; the hash value is linked to EP09 (BSA s.63(4)); the I4C line quotes the advisory's own scope (arrest on a
video call), not "never questioned on video"; the Saharanpur case is reported, with no names.
